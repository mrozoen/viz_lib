The log-likelihood, taken apart

Every step of the normal-error MLE derivation — plus the log rules, partial derivatives, and summation algebra that step depends on. Built around the exact problem that wrecked you.

The likelihood you were handed

\[\mathcal{L}(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)^2\right]\]

Nothing about this is hard once it's a sum instead of a product. That's what the log is for.

The five-move template

Maximum likelihood problems all have the same skeleton. If you know the five moves, the only thing that changes between problems is the algebra in the middle.

  • Write the likelihood. Usually a product over the n observations, because they're independent. Here it's already been multiplied out for you.
  • Take the natural log. Products become sums, exponents come down in front, and the exp vanishes. This is the whole trick.
  • Differentiate once with respect to each parameter. One partial derivative per unknown — here that's three: \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\), \(\sigma^2\).
  • Set each partial to zero and solve. The resulting system is the score equations. For regression they're literally the normal equations.
  • Check it's a max, not a min or a saddle. Second derivatives. Your problem says you can skip this — section 08 does it anyway, because your course will want it later.
The one thing to internalize

\(\ln\) is strictly increasing. So whatever value of \(\beta_1\) makes \(\mathcal{L}\) biggest also makes \(\ln\mathcal{L}\) biggest — the location of the maximum doesn't move, only the height does. That's why "maximize the log-likelihood" is a legal substitute for "maximize the likelihood," and it's worth one sentence of justification if your professor is strict.

Log rules, and the one that does all the work

Six rules. You need four of them in this problem. Throughout, \(\ln\) means \(\log_e\), and everything inside a log is strictly positive (which is fine — \(\sigma^2 \gt 0\) and \(2\pi \gt 0\)).

Reference
RuleWhat it buys you here
\(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\)Splits the constant from the exponential. Turns a product of \(n\) densities into a sum of \(n\) log-densities.
\(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)=\ln a-\ln b\)Handles the \(\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}}\) out front: \(\ln 1 - \ln(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}\).
\(\ln(a^{\,b})=b\ln a\)Brings \(n/2\) down in front. This is why the answer has \(-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2)\) in it.
\(\ln(e^{\,x})=x\)The big one. The entire \(\exp[\cdots]\) block collapses to whatever was in the brackets.
\(\ln 1 = 0\), \(\ln e = 1\)Kills the numerator of the leading fraction.
\(a^{-b} = \dfrac{1}{a^{\,b}}\)Not a log rule, but it's how you'd rewrite \(\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}}\) as \((2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\) before logging.
Not a rule

\(\ln(a+b) \neq \ln a + \ln b\). There is no rule for the log of a sum. This matters because the exponent in your likelihood contains a sum — and it stays a sum. You never get to distribute a log across \(\sum(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i)^2\), and you never need to: \(\ln(e^x)=x\) hands you that sum untouched.

Applied to the leading constant

Work it one rule at a time so you can see where every term comes from:

\[\ln\!\left[\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}}\right]=\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2)\]

The last split uses \(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\) with \(a=2\pi\) and \(b=\sigma^2\). Splitting is optional but strongly recommended: it isolates the only piece that depends on \(\sigma^2\), so when you differentiate with respect to \(\sigma^2\) the \(\ln(2\pi)\) term visibly dies as a constant.

The four derivative rules you actually need

This derivation uses no trig, no products of functions, no quotient rule. Four rules cover it.

Reference
RuleWhere it fires
Power. \(\dfrac{d}{du}u^{k}=k\,u^{k-1}\)On the squared residual, \(k=2\).
Chain. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}f(u(x))=f'(u)\cdot u'(x)\)The residual \(u=Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\) is itself a function of \(\beta_0,\beta_1\). Forgetting the \(u'\) factor is the single most common error here.
Log. \(\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\ln\theta=\dfrac{1}{\theta}\)On \(-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2)\), differentiating with respect to \(\sigma^2\) itself.
Reciprocal / power again. \(\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\theta^{-1}=-\theta^{-2}\)On \(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\text{SSE}\), which is really \(-\frac{1}{2}\text{SSE}\cdot\theta^{-1}\).
Change of variable that makes \(\sigma^2\) painless

Let \(\theta=\sigma^2\). Not \(\sigma\) — \(\sigma^2\) itself is the parameter you're differentiating with respect to. Then \(\sigma^4=\theta^2\), the log-likelihood is \(-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\frac{n}{2}\ln\theta-\frac{1}{2\theta}Q\), and it's an ordinary one-variable calculus problem in \(\theta\). Substitute \(\sigma^2\) back at the end. Students who differentiate with respect to \(\sigma\) instead get an extra factor of \(2\sigma\) everywhere from the chain rule; the final answer survives, but the intermediate mess is where the errors live.

The chain-rule move, in slow motion

Write \(e_i=Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\). Then \(\partial e_i/\partial\beta_0=-1\) and \(\partial e_i/\partial\beta_1=-X_i\), because \(Y_i\) and \(X_i\) are fixed data, not variables. So:

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_0}\,e_i^{\,2}=2e_i\cdot\frac{\partial e_i}{\partial\beta_0}=2e_i\cdot(-1)=-2\!\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)\] \[\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_1}\,e_i^{\,2}=2e_i\cdot\frac{\partial e_i}{\partial\beta_1}=2e_i\cdot(-X_i)=-2X_i\!\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)\]

Those two lines are the entire calculus content of the \(\beta\) part of this problem. Everything else is bookkeeping.

Partial derivatives, without the mystique

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative with a rule attached: differentiate with respect to one variable and treat every other variable as a constant. The curly \(\partial\) is notation, not new mathematics.

In \(\ell(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2)\) there are three unknowns, so there are three partials. When you compute \(\partial\ell/\partial\beta_0\):

  • \(\beta_1\) and \(\sigma^2\) are constants. They ride along; they don't get differentiated.
  • \(Y_i\) and \(X_i\) are data — also constants. This is the point people trip on: you are not differentiating with respect to \(X\). \(X\) is a number you measured.
  • \(n\) and \(\pi\) are constants, so \(-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi)\) and \(-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2)\) both differentiate to \(0\).

Stacking the three partials into a column gives the gradient, \(\nabla\ell\). "Find the maximum" means "find where the gradient is the zero vector" — all three partials zero at the same time. That's why you get a simultaneous system rather than three independent answers, and why \(\hat\sigma^2\) ends up depending on \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\).

Notation you'll see

\(\dfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial \beta_0}\), \(\ell_{\beta_0}\), and \(\partial_{\beta_0}\ell\) all mean the same thing. The score function is the gradient of the log-likelihood, \(U(\boldsymbol\theta)=\nabla\ell\); "score equations" or "estimating equations" means \(\nabla\ell=\mathbf 0\). A second partial like \(\dfrac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\beta_0\partial\beta_1}\) means differentiate by \(\beta_1\), then by \(\beta_0\) — and for well-behaved functions like this one the order doesn't matter (Clairaut's theorem), which is why the Hessian comes out symmetric.

Summation algebra

More points get lost here than in the calculus. \(\sum\) obeys a small set of rules; memorize them and the last third of the problem becomes mechanical. All sums run \(i=1\) to \(n\).

Reference
IdentityNote
\(\sum (a_i+b_i)=\sum a_i+\sum b_i\)Sums distribute over addition.
\(\sum c\,a_i = c\sum a_i\)Constants pull out front. \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are constants with respect to \(i\).
\(\sum c = nc\)The trap. \(\sum \beta_0 = n\beta_0\), not \(\beta_0\).
\(\sum Y_i = n\bar Y\)Just the definition of the mean, rearranged. Use it to convert between \(\sum\) form and bar form.
\(\sum (X_i-\bar X)=0\)Deviations from the mean always cancel. Enormously useful for simplifying.
\(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\sum f_i = \sum \dfrac{\partial f_i}{\partial\beta}\)Differentiation passes through a finite sum. This is what lets you differentiate one generic term and re-attach the \(\sum\).

The two shorthand quantities

Your textbook almost certainly defines these; using them keeps the final answers short.

\[S_{xx}=\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=\sum X_i^2-n\bar X^2, \qquad S_{xy}=\sum(X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)=\sum X_iY_i-n\bar X\bar Y\]

The right-hand forms are the "computational" versions and they're what falls out of the algebra naturally, so recognize both. The equivalence is worth deriving once yourself — expand the square and use \(\sum X_i = n\bar X\).

The worked solution

Here's the whole problem, one move per row. Left column is what you write; right column is the rule that licenses it.

  1. Start from the likelihood.

    \[\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)^2\right]\]

    Given This is the joint density of \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) under \(Y_i \sim N(\beta_0+\beta_1X_i,\ \sigma^2)\), read as a function of the parameters instead of the data.

  2. Take the natural log of both sides and split it.

    \[\ell=\ln\mathcal{L}=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)^2\]

    Log rules \(\ln(a/b)\), then \(\ln(a^b)=b\ln a\), then \(\ln(ab)\) on the constant; \(\ln(e^x)=x\) releases the bracket untouched. Legal because \(\ln\) is strictly increasing, so the maximizer doesn't move.

  3. Differentiate with respect to \(\beta_0\).

    \[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_0}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum 2\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)(-1)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)\]

    Chain rule The first two terms are constants in \(\beta_0\), so they vanish. Power rule gives the \(2\), the inner derivative \(\partial e_i/\partial\beta_0=-1\) gives the sign flip.

  4. Set it to zero and clear the \(1/\sigma^2\).

    \[\sum\left(Y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1X_i\right)=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \sum Y_i = n\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\sum X_i\]

    \(\sum c = nc\) Multiplying by \(\sigma^2 \neq 0\) is harmless. Distributing the sum turns \(\sum\hat\beta_0\) into \(n\hat\beta_0\) — this is the step that most often gets dropped.

  5. Differentiate with respect to \(\beta_1\), set to zero.

    \[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum X_i\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \sum X_iY_i=\hat\beta_0\sum X_i+\hat\beta_1\sum X_i^2\]

    Chain rule Same shape as step 3, except the inner derivative is \(-X_i\), so an \(X_i\) rides along inside the sum.

  6. Solve the first equation for \(\hat\beta_0\).

    \[\hat\beta_0=\bar Y-\hat\beta_1\bar X\]

    Divide by \(n\) Using \(\frac1n\sum Y_i=\bar Y\) and \(\frac1n\sum X_i=\bar X\). Note the fitted line always passes through \((\bar X,\bar Y)\).

  7. Substitute into the second equation and collect \(\hat\beta_1\).

    \[\sum X_iY_i=(\bar Y-\hat\beta_1\bar X)\,n\bar X+\hat\beta_1\sum X_i^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \sum X_iY_i-n\bar X\bar Y=\hat\beta_1\left(\sum X_i^2-n\bar X^2\right)\]

    Algebra No calculus left. Just move the \(\hat\beta_1\) terms to one side and the rest to the other.

  8. Read off the slope.

    \[\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum X_iY_i-n\bar X\bar Y}{\sum X_i^2-n\bar X^2}=\frac{\sum(X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y)}{\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\]

    \(S\) identities The three forms are the same number. Which one you write depends on your textbook's convention.

  9. Differentiate with respect to \(\sigma^2\). Write \(\theta=\sigma^2\) and \(Q=\sum(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i)^2\).

    \[\ell=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\frac{n}{2}\ln\theta-\frac{Q}{2}\theta^{-1} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta}=-\frac{n}{2\theta}+\frac{Q}{2\theta^{2}}\]

    Log + power rules \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\ln\theta=\frac1\theta\) and \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\theta^{-1}=-\theta^{-2}\), and the second minus sign flips the term positive. \(Q\) is a constant with respect to \(\theta\).

  10. Set to zero and solve for \(\theta\).

    \[\frac{n}{2\theta}=\frac{Q}{2\theta^{2}} \;\Longrightarrow\; n\theta = Q \;\Longrightarrow\; \hat\sigma^2=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\left(Y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1X_i\right)^2=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n}\]

    Multiply by \(2\theta^2\) Valid since \(\theta \gt 0\). Plug in the \(\hat\beta\)s from steps 6 and 8 — \(\hat\sigma^2\) is defined in terms of the fitted residuals.

The three estimators \[\hat\beta_1=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}},\qquad \hat\beta_0=\bar Y-\hat\beta_1\bar X,\qquad \hat\sigma^2=\frac{1}{n}\sum\left(Y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1X_i\right)^2\]
The follow-up question your professor will ask

\(\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}}=\mathrm{SSE}/n\) is biased: \(E[\hat\sigma^2]=\frac{n-2}{n}\sigma^2\). It divides by \(n\) rather than by the residual degrees of freedom, because MLE makes no correction for the two parameters already estimated from the same data. The unbiased estimator your course will use everywhere else is \(\mathrm{MSE}=s^2=\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)\). Both are "correct answers" to different questions — MLE is not required to be unbiased.

Why the MLE and least-squares slopes are identical

You can show this the long way — derive both, compare — or the short way, in one line. Do the short way first, then show the work.

The one-line argument

Write \(Q(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i)^2\), the least-squares criterion. Then the log-likelihood is exactly

\[\ell(\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma^2)=\underbrace{-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2)}_{\text{free of }\beta_0,\beta_1}\;-\;\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,Q(\beta_0,\beta_1)\]

For any fixed \(\sigma^2\), the first two terms are constants, and \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\) is a positive multiplier. So maximizing \(\ell\) over \((\beta_0,\beta_1)\) is the same as minimizing \(Q\) over \((\beta_0,\beta_1)\) — maximize a negative multiple of \(Q\), minimize \(Q\). Same optimization, same argmin, same estimators. No calculus needed for the argument itself.

The side-by-side, if you're asked to show work

Least squares

\[\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\beta_0}=-2\sum\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)\] \[\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\beta_1}=-2\sum X_i\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)\]

Set both to zero, divide by \(-2\).

Maximum likelihood

\[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial\beta_0}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)\] \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial\beta_1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum X_i\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)\]

Set both to zero, multiply by \(\sigma^2\).

The two columns differ only by the factor \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\) versus \(\tfrac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\) — both nonzero constants that get cleared when you set the expression to zero. Multiplying an equation by a nonzero constant doesn't change its roots, so both routes land on the same pair of normal equations:

\[\sum Y_i = n\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\sum X_i, \qquad \sum X_iY_i=\hat\beta_0\sum X_i+\hat\beta_1\sum X_i^2\]

and therefore on the same \(\hat\beta_0\) and \(\hat\beta_1\). \(\blacksquare\)

What is not the same

\(\sigma^2\) has no least-squares analogue — least squares never assumed a distribution, so it has nothing to say about the error variance. That's the real content of "normality buys you more": same point estimates for the slope and intercept, plus an estimator for \(\sigma^2\), plus the sampling distributions that make \(t\)-tests, \(F\)-tests, and confidence intervals valid.

Second-order conditions

Your problem waived this. Here's how it goes anyway, because "verify it's a maximum" shows up on later problem sets and on the multiple-regression version.

For a function of one variable, \(f''\lt 0\) at a critical point means maximum. For two or more variables you need the Hessian — the matrix of all second partials — and you check its definiteness. For \(Q(\beta_0,\beta_1)\):

\[\frac{\partial^2 Q}{\partial\beta_0^2}=2n,\qquad \frac{\partial^2 Q}{\partial\beta_1^2}=2\sum X_i^2,\qquad \frac{\partial^2 Q}{\partial\beta_0\partial\beta_1}=2\sum X_i\] \[\mathbf{H}_Q=\begin{pmatrix} 2n & 2\sum X_i\\[4pt] 2\sum X_i & 2\sum X_i^2\end{pmatrix}\]

The two-variable second-derivative test: a critical point is a minimum when the top-left entry is positive and the determinant is positive. Here the top-left entry is \(2n \gt 0\), and

\[\det\mathbf{H}_Q = 4\left(n\sum X_i^2-\left(\sum X_i\right)^2\right)=4n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=4n\,S_{xx}\;\gt\;0\]

which is strictly positive as long as the \(X_i\) are not all identical. So \(Q\) is strictly convex and the critical point is a genuine minimum — a global one, since \(Q\) is a quadratic. And because \(\ell = \text{const} - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}Q\) with \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\gt 0\), the Hessian of \(\ell\) in \((\beta_0,\beta_1)\) is \(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\mathbf{H}_Q\): negative definite, hence a maximum.

Why "not all \(X_i\) identical" is the condition

If every \(X_i\) equals \(\bar X\), then \(S_{xx}=0\), the determinant is zero, and \(\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) is \(0/0\) — undefined. Geometrically: all your data sit on one vertical line and infinitely many lines fit equally well, so there's no unique slope. This is the simple-regression version of the multicollinearity condition you'll meet in matrix form as "\(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) must be invertible" / "\(\mathbf X\) must have full column rank."

For \(\sigma^2\), the one-variable test suffices. At \(\theta=\hat\sigma^2 = Q/n\):

\[\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\theta^2}=\frac{n}{2\theta^{2}}-\frac{Q}{\theta^{3}} \;\Big|_{\,\theta=Q/n}\;=\;\frac{n^3}{2Q^2}-\frac{n^3}{Q^2}=-\frac{n^{3}}{2Q^{2}}\;\lt\;0\]

Negative, so it's a maximum. Note that the whole Hessian of \(\ell\) is block-diagonal here — the cross-partials \(\partial^2\ell/\partial\beta_j\partial\sigma^2\) vanish at the solution, because they're proportional to \(\sum e_i\) and \(\sum X_ie_i\), which are exactly the two things the normal equations set to zero. That block-diagonality is why \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) and \(\hat\sigma^2\) turn out to be independent, which in turn is what makes the \(t\)-statistic a \(t\).

Where this comes back in the rest of the course

Logs and partial derivatives are not a one-problem detour. They're the backbone of the second half of essentially every regression course.

Matrix calculus, immediately

Multiple regression redoes this derivation in vector form. The criterion becomes \(Q=(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta)'(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta)\) and you'll need two matrix derivative facts, which are the exact analogues of the power and chain rules you just used:

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol\beta}\,\mathbf a'\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf a, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol\beta}\,\boldsymbol\beta'\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta=2\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta \ \ (\mathbf A \text{ symmetric})\] \[\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\boldsymbol\beta}=-2\mathbf X'(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf X'\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X'\mathbf y\]

Same five moves, denser notation. \(\mathbf X'\mathbf X\) is the matrix version of the \(\begin{pmatrix}n & \sum X_i\\ \sum X_i & \sum X_i^2\end{pmatrix}\) you saw in the Hessian above — that's not a coincidence, it's literally \(\tfrac12\mathbf H_Q\).

Model comparison runs entirely on log-likelihoods

  • Likelihood ratio tests. The statistic is \(-2\ln\Lambda = -2\left(\ell_{\text{reduced}}-\ell_{\text{full}}\right)\), compared to a \(\chi^2\). The log is what makes the difference of logs a ratio of likelihoods.
  • AIC and BIC. \(\mathrm{AIC}=-2\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})+2p\) and \(\mathrm{BIC}=-2\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})+p\ln n\). You evaluate the log-likelihood at the MLEs — the expression you just derived — and add a penalty. Every model-selection table in your course is built on this.
  • Deviance. For GLMs, \(D = -2\left(\ell_{\text{model}} - \ell_{\text{saturated}}\right)\). Same machinery.

Logistic regression, where the closed form disappears

Same template, harder algebra. The log-likelihood for binary \(Y_i\) with \(p_i=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta_i}}\), \(\eta_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i\), is

\[\ell(\boldsymbol\beta)=\sum\left[\,Y_i\ln p_i+(1-Y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\,\right]=\sum\left[\,Y_i\eta_i-\ln\!\left(1+e^{\eta_i}\right)\right]\]

That simplification is pure log rules again. Setting \(\partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf 0\) gives \(\sum X_i(Y_i-p_i)=0\) — beautifully parallel to the normal equations, but \(p_i\) depends on \(\boldsymbol\beta\) nonlinearly, so there's no algebraic solution. That's when you need Newton–Raphson, and Newton–Raphson requires the Hessian. This is the point in the course where second derivatives stop being optional.

Log transformations of the variables themselves

A separate use of logs, and a favorite exam question. If you fit \(\ln Y = \beta_0+\beta_1X+\varepsilon\), then \(\beta_1 = \partial(\ln Y)/\partial X = \frac{1}{Y}\cdot\frac{\partial Y}{\partial X}\) — a relative change. A one-unit increase in \(X\) multiplies \(Y\) by \(e^{\beta_1}\), which for small \(\beta_1\) is approximately a \(100\beta_1\%\) change. In the log–log model \(\ln Y=\beta_0+\beta_1\ln X\), \(\beta_1\) is an elasticity: a 1% change in \(X\) gives roughly a \(\beta_1\)% change in \(Y\). Box–Cox transformations generalize this and are themselves chosen by — of course — maximizing a log-likelihood over the transformation parameter.

Weighted and generalized least squares

When \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_i)=\sigma^2/w_i\), the same derivation with the weights carried through gives \(Q_w=\sum w_i(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i)^2\). Nothing new — the \(w_i\) ride along as constants through every chain-rule step.

Mistakes to check for before you hand it in

  • Differentiating with respect to \(\sigma\) instead of \(\sigma^2\). The parameter is \(\sigma^2\). Substituting \(\theta=\sigma^2\) makes this impossible to get wrong.
  • Losing the inner derivative. \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_1}e_i^2\) is \(-2X_ie_i\), not \(2e_i\). Missing the \(-X_i\) is the most common single error in this derivation.
  • Writing \(\sum\beta_0=\beta_0\). It's \(n\beta_0\). \(\beta_0\) is inside the sum \(n\) times.
  • Treating \(\ln(a+b)\) as \(\ln a+\ln b\). There is no such rule; you never need one.
  • Sign slips on \(-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2)\) and \(-\frac{Q}{2}\theta^{-1}\). The second term differentiates to \(+\frac{Q}{2\theta^2}\) — two negatives. If your \(\hat\sigma^2\) comes out negative, this is where to look.
  • Forgetting to say why the log is allowed. One sentence: \(\ln\) is strictly increasing, so it preserves the location of the maximum. Cheap marks.
  • Not plugging the \(\hat\beta\)s into \(\hat\sigma^2\). \(\hat\sigma^2\) is a function of the fitted residuals, so it must be written with hats: \(\frac1n\sum(Y_i-\hat\beta_0-\hat\beta_1X_i)^2\).
  • Claiming \(\hat\sigma^2\) is unbiased. It isn't. If the question asks for an unbiased estimator, that's \(\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)\).

Drills

Work each one on paper before opening the answer. If you can do all seven cold, this problem type is finished for you.

01Expand \(\ln\!\left[(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\right]\) into three separate terms.
\[-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)=-\frac{n}{2}\ln 2-\frac{n}{2}\ln\pi-\frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2)\]

In practice you'd stop at \(-\frac n2\ln(2\pi)-\frac n2\ln(\sigma^2)\), since \(\ln(2\pi)\) is a single constant. Splitting it further is legal but pointless.

02Differentiate \(f(\theta)=-\frac{n}{2}\ln\theta-\frac{A}{2\theta}\) with respect to \(\theta\), and solve \(f'(\theta)=0\).
\[f'(\theta)=-\frac{n}{2\theta}+\frac{A}{2\theta^{2}}=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \hat\theta=\frac{A}{n}\]

With \(A=\mathrm{SSE}\) this is exactly the \(\hat\sigma^2\) result. Recognizing this shape saves you time on every variance MLE you'll ever do — Normal, exponential, Poisson-with-offset.

03Compute \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta_1}\sum(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i)^2\).
\[\sum 2\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)\cdot(-X_i)=-2\sum X_i\left(Y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1X_i\right)\]

Check yourself: is there an \(X_i\) inside the sum? If not, you differentiated with respect to \(\beta_0\) by mistake.

04Prove \(\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)=0\).
\[\sum(X_i-\bar X)=\sum X_i-\sum\bar X=n\bar X-n\bar X=0\]

The move is \(\sum\bar X = n\bar X\) — \(\bar X\) is a constant with respect to \(i\), so summing it \(n\) times multiplies it by \(n\). Same rule as \(\sum\beta_0=n\beta_0\).

05Is \(\ln(a+b)=\ln a+\ln b\)? Justify with a specific counterexample.

No. Take \(a=b=1\): the left side is \(\ln 2\approx 0.693\), the right side is \(0+0=0\). The correct identity, \(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\), applies to a product. A single counterexample is a complete disproof — you don't need a general argument.

06You fit \(\ln Y=3.2+0.08X\). Interpret the slope in plain language.

A one-unit increase in \(X\) multiplies \(Y\) by \(e^{0.08}\approx 1.083\) — about an 8.3% increase. The quick approximation \(100\times 0.08 = 8\%\) is close because \(e^{x}\approx 1+x\) for small \(x\); state the exact version if the coefficient is large, since the approximation degrades fast past roughly \(|\beta|=0.25\).

07Show in one line that the MLE and least-squares estimators of \(\beta_1\) must agree, without computing either.

\(\ell = c(\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}Q(\beta_0,\beta_1)\), where \(c\) doesn't involve the \(\beta\)s and \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\gt 0\). Maximizing \(\ell\) over the \(\beta\)s is therefore identical to minimizing \(Q\) over the \(\beta\)s, and \(Q\) is the least-squares criterion. Same optimization problem, same solution.
